The Problem
Stroke is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, yet many contributing risk factors remain poorly understood in combination. Clinical datasets offer the chance to surface patterns that individual analysis misses.
02
The Approach
A Flask-based clinical decision support tool was built on 172,000 simulated patient records. SQL queries power 11 targeted analyses across lifestyle, demographic, and physiological dimensions, each returning charts and plain-English insights.
03
The Outcome
Hypertension and heart disease emerged as the strongest comorbidities, each carrying roughly 10% stroke rates. Counter-intuitively, sleep duration and diet type showed minimal predictive signal, challenging common assumptions about lifestyle risk.

Key Findings

What the Data Revealed

Six insights drawn from 11 analyses across 172,000 patient records.

01
Hypertension is the strongest comorbidity
Of 25,823 hypertensive patients, 9.8% had a stroke — 2,522 individuals. This is the largest absolute count of any risk group and positions hypertension as the primary clinical warning sign.
02
Heart disease compounds the risk
10.1% of heart disease patients experienced a stroke. Mean glucose in this group was 183.53 mg/dL, pointing to elevated glucose as a key comorbidity alongside cardiac conditions.
03
Age skews older in high-risk groups
Among smokers with hypertension who had a stroke, the mode age was 73, well above the mean of 54.68. Older patients carry a disproportionate burden within already high-risk groups.
04
Diet type has no predictive signal
Mixed, Non-Vegetarian, and Vegetarian diets all showed a 10% stroke rate. Dietary category alone does not differentiate stroke risk, suggesting diet quality matters more than broad dietary labels.
05
Sleep duration is not a differentiator
The difference in average sleep hours between stroke and non-stroke patients was just 0.01 hours. Sleep quantity alone should not be used as a standalone stroke risk indicator.
06
Urban and rural risk profiles are similar
Urban stroke patients had a mean age of 54.19 vs 53.93 in rural areas. Geography does not appear to be a major independent driver of stroke risk in this dataset.
